## 1. Clean narration

> If you ever fake a number, there's a way to tell a human made it up. A man stole $2 million with fake checks,
> made every amount look totally random, and still got caught. But how? Well, picture a building with 19 floors.
> Step into the elevator and look at the buttons. Floors two through nine each start with a different number,
> but floor one and 10 all the way up to 19 all start with a one. That's more than half the buttons.
> Real numbers work like that elevator. So, ones show up way more than anything else. On top of that, real
> randomness is streaky. Flip a coin 100 times, and you'll usually get six heads in a row. His checks mostly
> began with a seven, eight, or nine, and he avoided doubles to make them look random. But truly random numbers
> are the opposite. So, no matter how random you try to make yours, there's almost always a way to tell.

Topic: Benford's law (leading-digit distribution) + streakiness of real randomness, told as a mini crime story
(embezzler Rico vs. auditor Marge). The last line repeats the first ("a way to tell") so the Short loops.

## 4. Narration → visual rules seen in this video
- Every noun with a number gets a physical, diegetic counterpart (cheque amount, LED counter, floor buttons, number buildings, coin board, cheque stacks, wall scribbles). Numbers are never floating motion-graphics — they live on props.
- Each sentence = one location (bank, car, police car, tower, elevator, sky, office, desk, window, jail) — locations change roughly every 3–4 s, faces change every 0.5–1.5 s.
- Face swaps land on the stressed word: "a **human**" (#77), "got **caught**" (#283), "**different**" (#527), "**always**" (#1379).
- The two characters carry the story arc (criminal → caught) while the narrator explains; there is no dialogue or lip sync.
